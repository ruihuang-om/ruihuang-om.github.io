As intermediaries between producers and consumers, electricity retailers must set competitive prices in the retail market, buy electricity from the spot market, and manage risk through the forward market. Despite ongoing liberalization efforts, low consumer switching rates and the dominant market share of incumbent retailers remain two key obstacles to retail electricity market liberalization, yet they are underexplored. To fill this gap, we develop a game-theoretical model that incorporates consumer switching costs and retail asymmetry, and examine their impacts on retail competition and the interactions among forward, retail, and spot markets. Our results show that greater retail asymmetry directly intensifies retail competition while higher switching costs diminish it, and these effects are indirectly amplified by the forward market. Greater retail competition not only fosters forward commitments but also stimulates spot production. Counterintuitively, weaker retailers, despite having smaller market shares and lower retail prices, may earn higher expected profits than dominant ones through forward trading. Electricity producers, in contrast, may gain or lose from higher retail asymmetry and switching costs. Finally, while higher switching costs always harm consumer surplus and social welfare, greater retail asymmetry, despite benefiting consumers, may adversely affect social welfare. These findings offer valuable insights for recent initiatives aimed at reducing switching costs and shortening retail contract durations.
